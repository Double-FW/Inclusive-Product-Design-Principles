# Inclusive Product Design Principles

This repository brings together interpretive design principles, first-person perspective models, and practical UX research guidance. It helps teams examine how products support meaningful tasks across different ways of perceiving and interacting.

The material is a set of lenses for human-centred design and research. It is not a usability standard, accessibility conformance scheme, or substitute for research with people.

## Who this is for

Designers, researchers, product teams, accessibility specialists, brand teams, and AI agents can use the material to:

- frame a task around the person’s goal and context;
- examine the cues, choices, feedback, and recovery a product provides;
- compare experiences across relevant interaction modes;
- identify whose perspective is missing and what evidence is needed;
- turn principles into bounded research questions and testable design proposals.

AI agents must follow [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md). The agent file sets source, evidence, privacy, authority, and human-review boundaries; this README describes the repository and its documents.

## How to use the material

Start with the document that matches the question. Use the Rams adaptation to frame design discussion, the indifference essay to challenge transferred costs, the perspective models to generate research questions, and the practice guide to plan evaluation or co-design.

Treat first-person “I” accounts as authored analytical models unless a source explicitly records participant provenance and method. They can prompt questions; they do not establish group preferences, prevalence, or usability. Do not imitate disability as a substitute for working with people who use the relevant methods.

Keep these evidence types distinct:

- **Source guidance:** what a cited source says.
- **Observation:** what happened in a specified task or context.
- **Participant report:** what a participant said about their experience.
- **Interpretation:** a possible explanation of evidence.
- **Hypothesis:** a claim that still needs checking.
- **Synthetic planning material:** generated scenarios or perspectives, not research evidence.

Choose a method that can answer the question. Small formative studies can reveal issues and help improve a design, but do not establish how common an issue is. Surveys, analytics, experiments, standards reviews, and assistive-technology testing each have different limits. Do not require a multivariate experiment for every usability issue or use satisfaction as a proxy for task success or accessibility.

## Functional experience and emotional interpretation

Task outcomes and emotional experience are related areas of inquiry, but they are distinct. A completed task does not by itself show confidence, trust, or satisfaction; a reported feeling does not establish task success, security, accessibility, or conformance. Do not state a causal relationship unless the evidence and method support it.

The POUR perspective documents use phrases such as “I feel seen”, “I feel capable”, “I feel confident”, “I feel safe”, and “I feel valued” as proposed research or brand-experience lenses. They do not redefine WCAG’s four principles—Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, and Robust—or create a fifth WCAG principle. Check [Terminology and Principles](https://github.com/Double-FW/Terminology-and-Principles) and its [current position](https://github.com/Double-FW/Terminology-and-Principles/blob/main/START_HERE.md) for the status and scope of Double-FW terminology. Approval of selected decisions there is not blanket approval of every glossary entry.

In particular, feeling safe does not demonstrate that a service is secure, and feeling confident does not establish understanding or successful task completion. Preserve distinctions such as customer/user/participant, observation/interpretation/estimate/hypothesis, and comparable/equivalent.

## Perspective and scope

“Sighted”, “nonvisual”, “visual-first”, and “audio-first” are analytical lenses, not fixed user categories. People may change modes by task or context. Nonvisual interaction can include screen readers, Braille, keyboard, voice, touch, haptics, or combinations. A nonvisual account does not cover all blind people, and neither it nor a sighted account represents a population.

These documents do not replace research with people with varied disabilities, access needs, devices, languages, cultures, or circumstances. State which perspectives are represented, what is missing, and what evidence would be needed for a consequential decision.
