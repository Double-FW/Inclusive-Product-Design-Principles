# Indifference: the cardinal sin in design

## Exploring Dieter Rams’s warning through his ten principles

> “Indifference towards people and the reality in which they live is actually the one and only cardinal sin in design.”
>
> — Dieter Rams

Rams’s sentence gives the ten principles a human centre. It asks us to look beyond whether a product is elegant, novel or well made and consider what its design says about the people who have to live with it.

Indifference is not always hostility, carelessness or a lack of good intention. It can appear in thoughtful teams and polished products. It happens when people’s circumstances are treated as inconvenient exceptions; when a design decision is defended by what it looks like to its makers instead of what it enables for its users; or when no one takes responsibility for the effort, uncertainty or exclusion the product creates.

In digital products, indifference can be visual, auditory, tactile, linguistic, temporal and economic. It can hide in a beautiful screen that makes the task harder, in a spoken interface that leaves out the brand’s meaning, in an update that discards learned behaviour, or in a service that works only with a fast connection and a new device. It can also be quiet: the missing confirmation, the unnamed control, the unexplained charge, the failure with no route back.

The following reading treats each of Rams’s ten principles as a way to notice and challenge indifference. It looks at functional consequences—whether people can understand and do what they came to do—and emotional consequences—whether the experience leaves people feeling capable, confident, respected, safe or unwelcome. These consequences belong together. A service that blocks a task also communicates something about how much the person matters.

This is a companion to *Ten principles for good digital product design: Reframing Dieter Rams for multimodal human experience*. It uses the same ten principles, but asks a different question of each: **What would indifference look like here, and what would attention to people require instead?**

---

## 1. Innovation should create meaningful capability

Indifference appears when novelty matters more to the organisation than the person’s ability to use the product. A team may celebrate a new gesture, an animated transition or a conversational interface without asking whether it removes a real difficulty. The design can be innovative in its construction and indifferent in its effect.

A visual-first experience may make a new pattern seem obvious because its movement or placement hints at what to do. Someone who cannot use that visual cue may have to guess, search or wait for instructions. An audio-first experience can make information newly available, but can also replace a quick overview with a long conversation, hide the available choices or make a mistaken action hard to reverse. In both cases, the burden of learning is passed to the person while the designer receives credit for novelty.

Careful innovation starts with a real human constraint. It asks who gains a new capability, what effort or risk is removed, who might pay a learning cost, and whether the person remains in control. It also treats people’s existing tools and habits as valuable knowledge rather than friction to be designed away.

**The human cost of indifference:** people become test subjects for an idea, and may lose time, confidence or privacy in the process.

**Questions to ask:**

- Whose problem does this innovation solve?
- What does it let someone do more easily, safely or independently?
- Can people discover, understand and reverse the new interaction?
- Have we tested the change with people whose circumstances differ from our own?

## 2. Usefulness is the primary test

Indifference appears when the organisation’s priorities replace the person’s purpose. The product may be useful to the business because it increases engagement, collects information or exposes more features, while the person struggles to pay a bill, find an answer, make a choice or finish an application.

The interface can be full of activity yet fail at the task that matters. A dashboard may promote secondary features over the action someone came to take. An audio-first user may be able to reach the service but still need another person to identify a control, check a total or explain whether a submission succeeded. That is not a small inconvenience: it can cost independence, time and privacy.

Care asks whether the product helps people reach their intended outcome with reasonable effort. It considers the full task, including preparation, review, confirmation and recovery. It treats features as valuable only when they serve a real need, and recognises that success is not the same as keeping a person inside the product for longer.

**The human cost of indifference:** people are treated as sources of clicks, data or revenue rather than as people trying to accomplish something.

**Questions to ask:**

- What did the person come here to do?
- Can they complete it without unnecessary steps or someone else interpreting the interface?
- Can they verify the result and recover from an error?
- Does the product’s success measure reflect the person’s outcome as well as the organisation’s?

## 3. Aesthetic quality should support use

Indifference appears when a design’s appearance is valued above the experience of the people receiving it. Visual polish can conceal a weak hierarchy, a hard-to-read typeface, an unclear control or a motion effect that delays the task. Distinctive brand expression can become a demand that people admire the work before they can use it.

The answer is not to dismiss visual design or to assume that a nonvisual experience has no aesthetic character. A visual-first person may encounter care through composition, type, contrast, imagery and rhythm. An audio-first person may encounter it through language, voice, sequence, pacing and the balance between useful information and repetition. For both, aesthetic quality grows from coherence and respect. It is not achieved by reproducing every visual detail in speech or by removing the character from an experience.

Care asks whose experience the aesthetic decision is serving. It allows richness, density and personality when they help the product communicate. It also accepts that the same brand can express itself differently across modes while preserving its meaning and character.

**The human cost of indifference:** a person is made to accommodate the designer’s taste, or is offered a functional but characterless version of the experience.

**Questions to ask:**

- Does the visual or nonvisual expression help people perceive meaning and feel the intended character?
- Which people are expected to adapt to the chosen style?
- Does the product remain coherent when the mode of interaction changes?
- Have we mistaken minimal appearance for ease of use?

## 4. The product should make itself understandable

Indifference appears when people are expected to know what the design team knows. A control may rely on an unfamiliar symbol, a heading may use internal language, an important condition may sit far from the choice it affects, or a selected state may be apparent only through colour. The interface asks people to reconstruct meaning from clues that were obvious only to its makers.

The assumptions differ by mode. Visual-first people may be expected to infer meaning from colour, position, resemblance or animation. Audio-first people may be expected to remember a two-dimensional layout they never received, or to navigate a long stream of unnamed objects. A service may be technically operable yet still leave someone unsure where they are, what changed or what will happen next.

Care makes meaning available at the point it is needed. It uses familiar language, useful structure and honest state changes. It does not remove explanation merely to preserve an illusion of simplicity. Good design reduces avoidable guessing; it does not make users feel foolish for failing to guess correctly.

**The human cost of indifference:** uncertainty becomes the user’s problem, and errors can feel like personal failure rather than a predictable result of unclear design.

**Questions to ask:**

- Can someone tell where they are, what they can do and what each choice means?
- Are labels clear outside the immediate visual context?
- Does the order of information support the order of the decision?
- After an action, does the product say what happened and what comes next?

## 5. The product should respect attention

Indifference appears when a product treats attention as something to capture rather than something a person chooses to give. Persistent prompts, autoplay, manipulative notifications, decorative motion, repeated requests and endless feeds can all compete with the person’s purpose.

This competition takes different forms. Visual-first users may have to sift through banners, overlays and competing calls to action. Audio-first users may be forced to listen to repeated labels, irrelevant announcements or interruptions that cannot be dismissed quickly. A quiet interface can also be indifferent if it fails to announce a meaningful status change, an error or the completion of an action.

Care means noticing that attention is finite and that people are often doing more than one thing. It lets people pause, stop, skip, dismiss and return. It makes interruptions proportionate to their importance. It provides enough information to keep someone oriented without making the product narrate itself continuously.

**The human cost of indifference:** people spend their attention serving the product, rather than using the product to serve their own purpose.

**Questions to ask:**

- Which messages genuinely help the person at this moment?
- Can people stop, dismiss, skip or return without losing their work?
- Is the experience quiet when nothing needs attention and clear when something changes?
- Are we measuring successful outcomes or merely time spent and repeated engagement?

## 6. The service should be honest

Indifference appears when the organisation benefits from a person misunderstanding the choice. The price may be technically disclosed but visually downplayed. A recurring charge may appear after an attractive offer. Refusal may take more effort than acceptance. An automated recommendation may look certain despite weak evidence.

A visual design can steer through emphasis, order, contrast and preselection. An audio-first experience can steer through the order in which options are spoken, unclear names or a cancellation route buried among unrelated controls. A brand voice can sound warm while the product withholds the information someone needs to make a real choice.

Care means explaining material terms and consequences before a decision. It makes uncertainty visible, distinguishes suggestion from fact, and respects a person’s choice to refuse or change their mind. Honesty is not only about avoiding false statements. It is also about not using the design to produce a false impression.

**The human cost of indifference:** the product converts a person’s trust, inattention or lack of context into an advantage for the organisation.

**Questions to ask:**

- Is important information available before the choice it affects?
- Are acceptance, refusal and cancellation comparably understandable and usable?
- Does the interface make automated or uncertain information clear?
- Would someone still choose this option if they understood the real terms?

## 7. The product should last

Indifference appears when change is made for the organisation’s sense of freshness while the cost of relearning is left to users. A redesign may move familiar actions, rename stable concepts or discard a route people have built into their habits. The product looks new to its makers; it feels unreliable to the people who depended on it.

A digital service also has a material life. It may stop working on a capable older device, remove a familiar input method, lose a person’s records or make export difficult. For someone who uses specialist tools or has carefully learned a particular interaction, a forced upgrade can mean more than replacing hardware: it can mean rebuilding a working environment.

Care does not freeze a product. It asks whether change solves a real problem, preserves useful knowledge and gives people a path to adapt. It considers how long content, links, data and interaction patterns remain useful. Longevity is a relationship with people’s time and investment, not merely a visual style that stays fashionable.

**The human cost of indifference:** the organisation externalises the burden of change and treats people’s learned knowledge as disposable.

**Questions to ask:**

- What knowledge or habit will this change disrupt?
- Does the update solve a problem people actually experience?
- Can people retain their work, history and familiar routes?
- Will the service continue to work on devices and tools that are still useful?

## 8. Care should reach every detail

Indifference appears when a team cares about the demonstration but not the real journey. The happy path may be polished while loading, empty, error, timeout, interrupted and recovery states remain unexplained. Unusual names, long content, translated text or a poor connection reveal that someone’s reality was never included in the design.

The details vary by mode. Visual-first people may lose their place when a layout changes, or miss an error hidden outside the current view. Audio-first people may have no clear announcement that a panel opened, no route back to the field with an error, or no confirmation that work was saved. When something goes wrong, the product may leave the person to diagnose both the problem and the design.

Care asks what happens before, during and after the ideal interaction. It designs for interruption, variation and recovery. It gives people enough information to correct a problem without blame and preserves their work wherever possible. Thoroughness is a way of showing that the person’s time and effort matter even when the team is not watching.

**The human cost of indifference:** a single failure can erase work, dignity and trust built across an otherwise successful journey.

**Questions to ask:**

- What happens when there is no data, a slow response or a failed connection?
- Can people find an error, understand it and recover without starting again?
- Do names, lengths, languages and input methods change how the product behaves?
- Have we designed the ending, confirmation and return journey as carefully as the first screen?

## 9. The product should consider its environmental cost

Indifference appears when digital products are treated as immaterial. Every service depends on devices, networks, data centres, energy and human attention. Heavy pages, unnecessary video, constant background activity, repeated downloads and computation without user value have costs that may be invisible in the interface.

Those costs also reach people unevenly. A large asset may be a minor delay on a fast connection and a serious barrier on a limited one. A demanding animation may drain the battery of an older phone. An update may make a still-capable device unusable. Requiring constant connectivity may make an ordinary task unavailable to someone in a different place or circumstance.

Care asks whether each resource cost gives the person a meaningful benefit. It seeks lighter journeys, fewer unnecessary transfers, efficient media and sensible support for existing devices. It also recognises that attention, time and energy are human resources. A product that keeps people scrolling or listening without purpose is not neutral simply because the screen is digital.

**The human cost of indifference:** waste is displaced onto the environment and onto people with less bandwidth, older devices, less time or less battery to spare.

**Questions to ask:**

- Which assets, effects, background tasks or model calls genuinely help the person?
- Does the service preserve progress and work under a weaker connection?
- Are people pushed to replace a device that could otherwise meet their needs?
- Does increased engagement create value for the person or only for the organisation?

## 10. Use as little design as possible

Indifference can disguise itself as minimalism. A label is removed because it looks cleaner. A confirmation disappears because it adds a step. An explanation is omitted because the designer expects people to infer meaning from an icon, colour or position. The interface becomes visually quieter while the person is asked to do more interpretation.

The reverse can happen too. An audio-first experience may describe every visible element, including decoration, and call the result equivalent because more information was provided. But more words can bury the meaning just as surely as too few. Both excess and omission can be indifferent when they ignore what the person needs in context.

Careful simplicity removes friction, repetition and distraction while protecting meaning, choice, feedback and recovery. It does not ask how little can be shown or said. It asks what is essential for this person to understand, decide and act—and what can be safely left out. “Less, but better” is a discipline of attention to people, not an aesthetic excuse for withholding information.

**The human cost of indifference:** a person must either work around missing meaning or endure noise the design team could have removed.

**Questions to ask:**

- What can we remove without making someone guess, remember or seek help?
- What needs to remain visible, spoken, labelled or available for later review?
- Does the design reduce effort for the person, or merely reduce elements for the designer?
- Is each simplification still clear across modes and circumstances?

---

## What the ten principles ask of us

Read through the lens of indifference, the principles are not ten separate styling instructions. Together they ask designers to keep attending to people: to their purposes, different ways of receiving meaning, need for control, limited time and resources, changing circumstances and right to be treated with dignity.

This attention cannot be proven by a statement of intent. It becomes credible through the experience a product repeatedly creates. People feel considered when they can act, understand, recover and rely on the service without having to conceal their needs or apologize for their circumstances. They feel disregarded when preventable barriers are noticed only after the product has already failed them.

Indifference is therefore a design risk that can be challenged at every stage. Ask who carries the cost of a decision. Ask whose reality has been treated as the default. Ask what the product communicates through its behaviour, not only through its words. Then change the design while the person’s experience is still within reach.
