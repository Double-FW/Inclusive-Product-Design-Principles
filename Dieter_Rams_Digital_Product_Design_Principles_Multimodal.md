# Ten principles for good digital product design

## Reframing Dieter Rams for multimodal human experience

Dieter Rams developed his ten principles in relation to physical products. This document carries their underlying questions into websites, apps and digital services. It treats the principles as prompts for design judgement, not as a checklist or a claim that a product is good because it satisfies a set of rules.

Digital products are experienced through more than a screen. People may take in an interface visually, listen to it, navigate it with a keyboard, explore it by touch, read it in Braille, speak to it, or move between these modes. The same person may change modes as their context changes. A useful design therefore considers not only what information is present, but how meaning, choice, feedback and emotion travel through different forms of interaction.

The two perspectives used here are **visual-first** and **audio-first**. They describe ways of encountering a product, not fixed kinds of people. Audio-first is especially useful for asking what happens when a person cannot rely on visual arrangement and receives information over time. It does not represent every nonvisual experience: Braille, touch, keyboard use and personal combinations of modes matter too. Nor does visual-first mean that a person relies only on sight.

The purpose is human experience: whether a product helps people understand, decide and act; whether it preserves agency and dignity; and whether using it feels reassuring, frustrating, welcoming or exhausting. These are functional and emotional questions.

## A useful order for making design choices

When design goals compete, begin with the person’s intended outcome. Help them understand the choices and consequences, keep them in control, and make the service dependable over time. Then consider how restraint, aesthetic character and innovation can strengthen that experience. This order prevents novelty or visual polish from standing in for usefulness.

A calm screen that hides a charge is not simple in any meaningful sense. A spoken interface that names every object but makes the user listen through irrelevant detail is not clear. A striking interaction that is hard to predict can feel impressive to its maker and tiring to the person using it.

## 1. Innovation should create meaningful capability

**Digital principle:** Introduce new technology or interaction when it gives people a worthwhile new ability, removes friction or makes a difficult task easier. Novelty alone is not progress.

**Visual-first experience.** A new pattern may be easy to infer from animation, familiar imagery or spatial arrangement. That initial legibility can make an innovation feel natural, but it does not prove that the interaction is self-explanatory. If people must learn a designer’s gesture or wait through ornamental transitions to do an ordinary task, the design has added a learning cost. Innovation earns its place when it gives a visible, understandable advantage and lets people predict what will happen.

**Audio-first experience.** A new interaction may remove visual searching or make information available through conversation, sound or spoken control. It can also make choices harder to discover, require long listening, or leave people unsure what the system heard and did. Innovation should help people act with confidence, preserve privacy and offer a way to check or undo consequential actions. It should work with the tools and habits people already use where possible.

**Human experience to aim for:** progress that feels empowering rather than experimental at the user’s expense.


### Brand and customer experience connection

A brand is also experienced through the new capabilities its product makes possible. **Visual-first:** a distinctive interaction can express a brand’s character, but should make the benefit easy to see and predict. **Audio-first:** spoken guidance, sound and feedback should preserve that character while making the action discoverable and reversible. Innovation builds trust when it helps people feel capable across modes.

## 2. Usefulness begins with the person’s outcome

**Digital principle:** Judge a product by whether it helps people achieve their purpose accurately, confidently and with reasonable effort—not by how many features it contains.

**Visual-first experience.** A dashboard can look capable while burying the one fact or action someone came for. Promotions and secondary functions may compete with the main task. Visual hierarchy is useful when it reflects real importance: it helps people find what they need, understand the result and recover from mistakes.

**Audio-first experience.** Access to spoken content is not enough if people cannot find the task, understand their options, enter and review information, act and confirm the outcome. A service is less useful when a person must ask someone else to identify a control, check a total or explain an error. That dependence can cost time, privacy and confidence.

**Human experience to aim for:** the satisfaction of completing the task independently and knowing it is done.


### Brand and customer experience connection

Usefulness is where a brand promise becomes an outcome. **Visual-first:** the main customer task needs a clear place in the hierarchy. **Audio-first:** people should be able to find, complete and verify that task without relying on a sighted intermediary. A brand earns the feeling of capability when its service supports the person’s intention rather than merely promoting its features.

## 3. Aesthetic quality should be felt through the whole experience

**Digital principle:** Let aesthetic character grow from the quality of the experience. Beauty can support meaning, feeling and trust; it cannot compensate for confusion or poor function.

**Visual-first experience.** Typography, colour, imagery, spacing, movement and composition shape first impressions and help people perceive relationships. Their value lies in how they make priorities, content and feedback intelligible. Visual polish becomes hollow when it hides controls, weakens important distinctions or uses motion to delay. Aesthetic quality does not require minimalism: density can suit expert work, and richness can carry meaning when it remains coherent.

**Audio-first experience.** Aesthetic quality may be felt in the rhythm and order of information, the tone of language, the timing of feedback and the balance between context and repetition. A coherent experience can feel calm and considered. Repeated labels, irrelevant announcements and long stretches of undifferentiated speech create a kind of clutter in time. Merely describing a visual theme does not recreate its aesthetic value; the experience itself needs care.

**Human experience to aim for:** a sense that the product is coherent, considered and pleasant to use in the mode a person chooses.


### Brand and customer experience connection

Brand character travels through both appearance and behaviour. **Visual-first:** type, colour, imagery and composition can make an experience distinctive while helping people perceive what matters. **Audio-first:** language, voice, pacing and interaction rhythm can carry character without narrating every visual detail. Both perspectives need a coherent experience that feels considered, not a visual theme translated word for word.

## 4. The product should make itself understandable

**Digital principle:** Help people form a reliable mental model of where they are, what they can do, what a choice means and what will happen next.

**Visual-first experience.** People often infer structure from headings, grouping, position, shape and visual hierarchy. Those signals should agree with how the product behaves. A control that looks interactive should respond as expected; related information should appear close to the choice it explains. Clear labels and short explanations are not failures of simplicity. They can reduce the effort of guessing.

**Audio-first experience.** Meaning cannot depend on colour, placement, size or resemblance alone. People need useful names, relationships, states and actions, presented in a structure they can navigate. Signposts should reflect the real organization: too few can leave an undifferentiated stream, while too many create noise. Familiar behaviour matters too. If an object identifies itself as a button, field or link, its operation should match that identity.

**Human experience to aim for:** confidence that comes from understanding, rather than from trial and error.


### Brand and customer experience connection

A brand feels confident when its language and behaviour make sense together. **Visual-first:** hierarchy and typography help people understand relationships and sequence. **Audio-first:** meaningful names, ordered information and clear state changes do the same over time. In either mode, an expressive voice should not make prices, choices or consequences harder to understand.

## 5. The product should respect attention

**Digital principle:** Let people focus on their purpose. Make the product prominent when it has something useful to communicate and quiet when it does not.

**Visual-first experience.** Unnecessary prompts, decorative animation and competing calls to action can interrupt reading and decision-making. Yet restraint should not make important actions, warnings or changes hard to notice. A banking service, a medical result and a work tool each call for a tone and pace suited to their context.

**Audio-first experience.** Speech unfolds over time, so every unnecessary announcement adds listening and navigation effort. People benefit from concise language, direct routes, ways to bypass repetition and notifications proportionate to their urgency. But silence can be as confusing as noise when a price changes, a panel opens or an action succeeds. Remove noise while preserving evidence of meaningful change.

**Human experience to aim for:** calm focus, with timely information when it affects a person’s understanding or control.


### Brand and customer experience connection

A brand’s tone is partly the way it treats people’s attention. **Visual-first:** restrained composition lets the task breathe while useful hierarchy keeps important information prominent. **Audio-first:** concise labels, proportionate announcements and a calm listening rhythm prevent the service from becoming verbally intrusive. Readable text can make the brand feel considerate rather than demanding.

## 6. The service should be honest about choices and consequences

**Digital principle:** Make the real terms, trade-offs and effects of an action understandable before people choose. Do not use design to manufacture consent or urgency.

**Visual-first experience.** Emphasis, order and contrast can steer people without a direct falsehood. A prominent acceptance button beside a barely visible refusal, a preselected option or a countdown can turn scanning habits into pressure. People should be able to understand prices, recurring charges, data use, uncertainty and cancellation before committing.

**Audio-first experience.** Names, descriptions and announced states carry much of the surrounding context. A preferred option can be encountered first or made easier to select; important conditions can appear after the choice in the listening sequence. People should hear material consequences before acting, and be able to understand what is selected, what will be shared and how to reverse a decision.

**Human experience to aim for:** informed choice, including the genuine freedom to say no.


### Brand and customer experience connection

Trust depends on the gap between what a brand says and what its service does. **Visual-first:** emphasis, order and preselected choices should not disguise terms or pressure acceptance. **Audio-first:** names and spoken sequence should make the terms and option to refuse clear before commitment. The experience feels confident and respectful when brand voice, visual expression and actual consequences agree.

## 7. The product should preserve useful familiarity over time

**Digital principle:** Improve a service without needlessly destroying what people have learned, rely on or created within it.

**Visual-first experience.** Moving familiar actions, renaming concepts or replacing established patterns can make a redesign feel fresh to its authors and disorienting to its users. Stable visual relationships and language help people build skill. Change is worthwhile when it solves a real problem and is introduced in a way that lets people adapt.

**Audio-first experience.** People may invest substantial effort learning a service’s vocabulary, structure, shortcuts and routes. A change to names or sequence can remove that accumulated knowledge at once. Preserve dependable paths, saved position and choices, and familiar concepts where they still make sense. Explain changes that affect established behaviour.

**Human experience to aim for:** the reassurance of a service that improves while respecting people’s memory and investment.


### Brand and customer experience connection

People build a relationship with a brand through repeated use. **Visual-first:** a recognisable system can evolve without moving familiar actions for novelty’s sake. **Audio-first:** stable vocabulary, routes and control behaviour protect the knowledge people have built. Typography governance can preserve recognisable character while adapting to language, device and reading context.

## 8. Care should reach every state and detail

**Digital principle:** Design the whole journey, including waiting, failure, recovery and unusual situations. The edges of an experience are part of the product.

**Visual-first experience.** A polished main journey can still fail in loading, empty, error or confirmation states. Long content, zoomed layouts, interrupted sessions and uncommon choices reveal whether the design has been thought through. Dates, totals, labels and status need to remain consistent; visual refinement cannot redeem an incorrect result.

**Audio-first experience.** Names, reading order, focus, status and recovery may not be visible in the design files, but they are the interface a person receives. Consider what happens on arrival, after opening or closing a panel, when an error appears, when the connection fails, and when someone returns later. A person should be able to locate the problem, correct it and continue without losing their work or privacy.

**Human experience to aim for:** trust that the service has considered the real journey, not only its ideal demonstration.


### Brand and customer experience connection

People meet a brand throughout the journey, including moments not shown in a polished screen. **Visual-first:** loading, errors, empty states and recovery should retain clear hierarchy and tone. **Audio-first:** names, sequence, status and feedback should make those same moments understandable and recoverable. Care in these details is evidence that customers were considered throughout.

## 9. Reduce environmental and human waste

**Digital principle:** Use energy, data, devices and people’s attention with care. Avoid work and consumption that add little value.

**Visual-first experience.** Oversized media, automatic playback, constant background activity and effects that add weight without meaning can slow a service, drain a battery and exclude people on older devices or poor connections. A lighter product can feel faster and more dependable. Endless feeds and repeated prompts also consume attention as a resource.

**Audio-first experience.** Heavy downloads, repeated processing and forced listening make ordinary tasks costly in time, battery and data. Obsolete devices can carry a particular burden when replacing them also means relearning commands or rebuilding a familiar setup. Efficient services preserve progress, avoid unnecessary audio and computation, and remain useful across a reasonable range of devices and connections.

**Human experience to aim for:** a service that feels considerate of the resources people and the environment contribute.


### Brand and customer experience connection

A considerate brand avoids wasting people’s time, attention, data and device resources. **Visual-first:** large media and motion should earn the performance cost they impose. **Audio-first:** unnecessary playback, downloads and forced listening also have a cost. A lean type system and careful font delivery can preserve brand identity while helping the experience work well across devices.

## 10. Use as little design as the task allows—and no less meaning

**Digital principle:** Remove what does not help people understand, decide or act. Do not confuse simplicity with omission.

**Visual-first experience.** Fewer words, controls or visible elements do not automatically make a screen easier. Removing context, confirmation or recovery can make people think harder. Simplicity comes from prioritising well: reduce duplication, competing actions, unnecessary stages and decorative motion while retaining the information and control the task requires.

**Audio-first experience.** A person may need words for meaning that another person can infer from appearance or position. An unnamed control or unannounced state is not elegant minimalism; it transfers interpretive work to the user. Remove repetition and irrelevant speech, but provide enough context, structure, feedback and recovery for a person to act with confidence.

**Human experience to aim for:** the shortest clear route to understanding and control, with no needless friction and no missing meaning.

### Brand and customer experience connection

Restraint should remove friction without stripping away meaning or identity. **Visual-first:** fewer elements help only when hierarchy, useful labels and brand character remain. **Audio-first:** less repetition helps only when people still receive needed context, state and feedback; visual personality should be conveyed through meaningful language and interaction, not an inventory of colours and shapes. A well-governed type system can balance distinctiveness with readable everyday communication.

## A multimodal view of good digital design

These ten digital principles describe a connected human experience. Visual hierarchy can help someone scan and compare; audio can make information available while attention is elsewhere or when a screen is not the useful channel. Touch, Braille, speech and keyboard interaction each bring their own strengths. Good design lets the important meaning survive as people move between modes.

That does not mean making every mode identical. A spoken experience does not need to narrate every visual detail, and a visual experience does not need to imitate speech. The question is whether each mode communicates what a person needs to understand, choose, act and recover—and whether the transitions between modes preserve their sense of place and control.

Function and emotion are intertwined. A clear price supports a decision and reduces anxiety. A predictable response saves effort and builds trust. A graceful recovery can turn a mistake into a manageable moment. A product that respects attention can feel humane. A product that hides meaning can make people feel foolish, dependent or unwelcome, even when its authors intended a clean interface.

Good digital design is therefore more than an attractive screen or a spoken version of one. It is a deliberate relationship between a person’s purpose, the choices available, the way the product responds and the feeling left by that exchange. Rams’s principles remain useful when they help us ask whether that relationship is meaningful, honest, coherent and worth returning to.
